The Score Takes Care of Itself: Growing Product Judgement When AI Does the Work

Product-Led Summit San Francisco

AI has made shipping faster than ever, but the work it takes over is often where engineers used to build product judgment. Starting from Bill Walsh’s idea that the score takes care of itself, this talk looks beneath delivery metrics, with lessons from Pulley, Webflow, and Figma and four practices for AI-native teams. The score

01The Score Takes Care of Itself

Thanks for having me. I’m Marvin Guerra, Head of Engineering at Pulley.

One of the things I’m currently excited about is that the NFL season is back. And as I was preparing for my fantasy football drafts, I was reminded of one of the leadership books I keep coming back to: Bill Walsh’s The Score Takes Care of Itself.

That’s an interesting title for a football coach. Your job is to win games, and everybody can see the score. But he’s telling you to put your attention somewhere else. That idea has shaped how I think about leading an engineering team.

To understand what Walsh meant, we need to remember the team he was trying to rebuild.

021979: two wins, fourteen losses

Bill Walsh became head coach of the San Francisco 49ers in 1979. His first season ended with two wins and fourteen losses, the league’s worst record.

He had a team to rebuild, and the results weren’t going to turn around overnight. He needed to give people something they could work on every day, even while they were losing.

He called that his Standard of Performance. It set clear expectations for how people prepared, practiced, and did their jobs across the organization.

Winning was still the goal. The standard gave everyone a way to contribute to it before the wins started showing up.

03The process rather than the prize

His message: “Concentrate on what will produce results rather than on the results, the process rather than the prize.”

By the 1981 season, the 49ers were thirteen and three. They finished that season by winning Super Bowl XVI.

The scoreboard told Walsh whether the team had won. It couldn’t tell him what to work on at practice the next morning. His standard gave him a way to do that.

For an engineering team, part of that preparation is learning to make better product decisions.

04Judgment sits under the score

Can an engineer explain why a customer needs a change? Can they spot an assumption we haven’t tested? Can they tell when we should wait instead of build?

Those decisions shape the product long before a metric moves.

That’s what I mean by product judgment: using customer context and evidence to decide what’s worth building, what can wait, and what we should change when we learn something new.

At Pulley, I had to turn that idea into everyday habits.

05At Pulley, the standard came first

At Pulley, I inherited an organization with ninety-seven open bugs, a demanding on-call rotation, and teams struggling to deliver predictably. I focused on better planning, retrospectives with clear follow-up actions, and blameless post-mortems that addressed recurring problems.

As we made those changes, open bugs went from ninety-seven to five. On-call load dropped by more than ninety percent. More than eighty percent of commitments were delivered on time. Those numbers mattered. But each week, I was looking at how we planned, how we learned from mistakes, and whether people could make better decisions the next time.

That’s how Walsh’s idea showed up in my own work. The numbers improved because we changed those everyday habits.

Now AI is changing how we work again. I want my team using it. There’s real value in getting through repetitive work faster and having more time to try ideas. But it has made me ask which parts of the work were helping people learn, and what happens when AI takes over those parts.

Where do people get the reps?

06Output is easy to see

When a task that took days takes hours, the improvement is easy to see. An idea that sat in a backlog becomes something we get to try.

But some of the time we saved used to include investigating a problem, trying an approach, and getting feedback from a colleague. Some of that effort was waste. Some of it was how the person learned.

We see that the task took less time. What we don’t see is whether the engineer also lost a chance to understand the problem or learn from someone else’s feedback.

That’s why the Judgment tile is blank. Faster delivery doesn’t answer that question.

That leaves me with a question.

07If AI does more of the work, where do people get the reps?

If AI does more of the work, where do people get the reps?

Think about something you’re good at now. Where did you actually get good at it?

My favorite interview question is: “What is the biggest fire you’ve caused at a prior company?”

I’m listening for what they missed, how they responded, and what they do differently now. We don’t need to recreate the fire, but we do need chances to make decisions and learn from them.

MIT is asking a similar question about its students. A few weeks ago, MIT published a report on how AI is changing learning on campus.

08Cognitive surrender

MIT’s students told the committee they felt most tempted to hand the thinking over to AI when they were afraid of missing a deadline. That’s a situation every engineering team recognizes. You’re under pressure, and there’s a tool ready with an answer.

The report warns that relying too heavily on that answer may weaken learning. It points to early signs of harm to critical thinking, memory, and confidence. It describes “cognitive surrender”: turning to AI as soon as the work gets difficult.

MIT also reports fewer students attending office hours or joining online discussions. The committee heard accounts of fewer in-person study groups, too. That means fewer conversations where someone asks you to explain your thinking and helps you work through what you don’t understand.

The same risk shows up in engineering: the task gets done, but the engineer misses the practice of deciding how to solve it. What needs to stay in the work for people to keep learning?

09Three conditions support skill

I recently read a book called The Skill Code. In it the author describes three conditions that help people build skill: challenge, complexity, and connection.

Challenge means enough difficulty to stretch you. Complexity means understanding the wider context of the task. Connection means a relationship of trust and respect with someone more expert than you.

For product work, I would translate those into three questions. Did you have to make a decision? Did you understand the customer’s situation and how the system worked? Did someone help you examine your reasoning?

When we bring AI into the work, we still need people making decisions, understanding the customer, and discussing their reasoning with someone more experienced.

In engineering, that learning often happens in ordinary work.

10The small reps add up

Think about a newer engineer and an experienced colleague investigating a confusing support ticket together. They work out what the customer expected, figure out what happened, and discuss what to change. The newer engineer gets to make a first attempt and hear what their colleague notices.

Now imagine the senior engineer using AI to investigate and implement the fix alone. The work might be excellent. But the newer engineer never gets that experience.

Nobody decided to stop teaching. The work just stopped requiring that conversation.

So we have to make it happen on purpose. Ask the newer engineer to recommend a fix. Then talk through what they considered and what they might have missed.

That practice matters even more as AI makes building easier and leaves us with more choices about what to build.

Choosing what to build

11Choosing deserves more attention

When AI helps us build faster, we get to try more ideas. We still have to choose which ones deserve the effort.

That makes product judgment more important. We need to understand which problems matter to customers and which ideas are worth testing.

Shipping more features doesn’t guarantee we have solved the customer’s biggest problem.

Building a feature might be easy. Using the product afterward might be harder.

12More features can add more friction

A feature might take an afternoon to build. Customers still have to notice it, understand it, and decide when to use it.

Every extra choice asks the new user to make another decision. Every new setting gives them something else to figure out.

If the customer’s problem is getting started, adding more options can make that problem worse.

We need to find where they’re stuck and what would help them move forward. That gives us something specific to test, and a reason to build it.

At Webflow, understanding what new users were struggling with led us toward one specific thing worth building.

13Webflow: the blocker was layout

At Webflow, my team’s goal was to reduce the learning curve.

We used a four-week diary study and funnel analysis to understand where new users struggled. Creating layouts stood out. The research found that only one in ten could create even a basic layout.

We developed what became Quick Stack, a simpler layout building block. We asked participants to build five layouts. With the existing builder, none of the twenty-five participants finished all five. With the prototype, ten of the nineteen participants finished all five.

These were small tests with different participants, but they gave us enough evidence to keep going. After release, we saw a five percent relative increase in overall activation. The research helped us choose where to put our effort. Once we had chosen Quick Stack, engineers still had to make all the decisions that turned that idea into something people would use.

That’s where product judgment shows up every day.

14Product outcomes depend on small calls

An engineer rarely gets a ticket that answers every question.

They still have to decide what happens when a user skips a step, which setting should be the default, or whether another option will help someone.

Those choices shape the customer’s experience. A manager won’t be in the room for most of them.

We need engineers who understand the problem well enough to make those calls, and we need to give them practice doing it.

If the experienced people keep making the decisions while everyone else implements them, the team might keep delivering. But we’re relying on the judgment we already have, without developing it in more people.

That’s the risk I want us to pay attention to.

15Delivery can improve while judgment shrinks

Delivery can improve while opportunities to develop product judgment shrink.

That’s why we need to look beneath the score. A team keeps shipping, but fewer engineers get to weigh the options, hear from customers, or see what happened after launch.

Those are the experiences that help someone make a better call next time. If AI changes who does that work, we need to think about where those experiences will come from.

Use some of the time AI saves to give engineers more room to explore an idea, test their reasoning, and learn with someone more experienced.

Here are four ways to make that part of the work.

Four practices

16A standard for AI-native teams

The first three practices give engineers the challenge, complexity, and connection we talked about earlier. The fourth is about making that learning a regular part of the team’s work.

Let me show you what this looked like at Figma, starting with how we chose our first AI features.

17Practice 1: Stretch on purpose

At Figma, our Editor AI team worked on features that helped designers get through repetitive work faster. The clip shows examples like replacing placeholder content and renaming layers. The goal was productivity. But deciding which features to build also gave our engineers practice making product decisions.

When we were choosing those first features, we got together in person and brainstormed potential ideas. Then we let the engineers choose which ideas they wanted to build proofs of concept for. That gave them a decision they could get their arms around. They had to weigh what was useful, what was feasible, and how quickly we could build it. They presented their prototypes, and we decided together which ones should go into production.

The important part was that engineers made the first recommendation. AI could help them explore alternatives or test an assumption, but they still had to choose an approach and explain why. Give them room to make that call before you offer your own.

But choosing a feature was only the beginning.

18Practice 2: Keep the whole problem visible

Each engineer then owned a feature through development. A few weeks after launch, they came back to the team and presented what we had learned about usability and adoption.

They saw how customers actually used what they built. We used those findings to make changes or expand features that were getting strong adoption.

For your team, start that ownership with the customer’s problem and keep it going after launch. Engineers need to see what happened because of their decisions.

Use AI to summarize the feedback, but give engineers time to read customer comments or watch a usability session themselves. That is where they connect the summary to someone’s actual experience.

Seeing what happened is part of the learning. Talking it through with someone more experienced helps you notice things you missed.

19Practice 3: Protect connection

At Figma, getting together in person gave us time to work through ideas. Engineers presented their thinking, heard questions from the team, and helped decide what we would build. The value was in staying with a question long enough to understand each other’s reasoning.

In a design review, ask the engineer what they see and what they recommend first. Then explain what you noticed and why it matters.

AI is useful for getting another perspective. However, a colleague also knows the customer, the history of the product, and the consequences the team will have to live with. And they’re there after launch to help you understand what happened.

Being together in person gave us room for that back-and-forth. Remote teams need time for it too.

Once you find a way of learning that works, make room for it regularly.

20Practice 4: Make learning visible

After those launches, we made in-person brainstorms and hackathons a more regular practice. They had helped us build useful features and learn from each other.

Maker Week is a broader example from Figma of setting aside time to explore and learn together.

Give the ways people already learn from each other a regular place in the workday. A useful conversation should not depend on someone finding spare time.

One place to start is a short review of a decision made with AI: what did the tool suggest, what did we choose, and what did we learn?

Include good calls as well as mistakes.

That makes one person’s experience useful to the rest of the team.

These practices are worth thinking about before we automate the next task. That decision changes both how the work gets done and what someone learns by doing it.

Putting it into practice

21What does automating this take away from someone's growth?

When we make that decision, we already ask two questions: what should we automate, and where do we still need human involvement?

Here’s a third: what does automating this take away from someone’s growth?

Some tasks offer very little learning. Automating them gives people time for more useful work.

Other tasks give someone practice investigating a customer’s problem or choosing between possible solutions. If AI takes over that task, decide where the person will get that practice instead.

Keep the time savings, and make sure there’s still something for the person to work out.

Then check whether they’re actually learning from it.

22The score and what's underneath

Keep tracking delivery. Alongside it, look for evidence that people are still developing product judgment.

That might be an engineer spotting an assumption they would previously have missed, explaining a tradeoff more clearly, or changing their recommendation after hearing from a customer.

Those are signs of learning that a delivery metric won’t show you.

You don’t need a new team-wide process to start looking for this. Use a piece of work that’s already happening.

23One week to start

On Monday, choose one task where your team uses AI and give an engineer a real decision to own.

During the week, make time for them to work through it with an experienced colleague.

On Friday, review the decision together and agree on any follow-up after launch.

That’s a concrete way to put Walsh’s idea into practice: pay attention to how the work happens, as well as what gets delivered.

24The standard is our responsibility

I would love to hear how your team is approaching this. Connect with me on LinkedIn so we can keep talking.

Walsh told us The Score Takes Care of Itself. The standard was still his responsibility.

Delivery can improve while opportunities to develop product judgment shrink. Our job is to notice that and change how the work happens.

Keep the delivery gains. Make sure the next generation of engineers gets to build the judgment behind them.

That is our standard to set.
